How Leaf Doctor works a case

Models look, reason and write. Code makes the verdict and enforces the safety rules.

  1. Looks at your photos

    Plant, symptoms, where they sit, and whether the photo is good enough.

    How exactly?

    Up to three photos: a close-up, the whole plant, the underside. Nemotron 3.5 Super VL reads them, so every model Leaf uses is an NVIDIA one.

  2. Lists what it could be

    Every plausible cause, each with a probability and the evidence for and against.

    How exactly?

    NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Ultra, with reasoning on. No cause is allowed above 90%, because a photo is never proof.

  3. Investigates

    Looks closer, checks your weather, reads sources and can ask you up to three questions.

    How exactly?

    Nemotron Ultra calls tools: it crops a photo region and asks a targeted question, runs 14 days of your weather through published disease-risk rules (TOMCAST for tomato; Hutton, Smith and Wallin for potato), searches and reads extension pages with Tavily, and checks recent outbreak news. Code caps it at 8 tool calls, a dollar budget and 90 seconds. Tavily ranks .edu, .gov and extension sites first, and Leaf only reads pages its own searches returned.

  4. Gets a second opinion

    A different model sees only the evidence and argues for the strongest alternative.

    How exactly?

    NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Super. It never sees Leaf's answer. If it disagrees strongly, the confidence drops.

  5. Writes the plan, then checks it

    Every step must cite a passage Leaf read. Steps the source doesn't support are removed.

    How exactly?

    NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Lightning writes it. A claim check then drops unsupported steps and any dose the source never states, and every product is checked against a table of 33 active ingredients for organic status, pets and bees.

  6. Decides, in code

    Confident, Likely, or Send a sample to a lab.

    How exactly?

    From the numbers, not the model's wording: how far the leading cause is ahead, photo quality, the second opinion, and whether a serious disease is in play (backend/verdict.py).

Which diseases go to a lab first?

Ones where a wrong guess spreads past your garden: late blight, tomato brown rugose fruit virus, sudden oak death, citrus greening, Ralstonia race 3 biovar 2, boxwood blight, spotted lanternfly and box tree moth. They go to a lab when they're in the top two causes (at 15% or more) or reach 20% anywhere, second opinion included.

After the diagnosis

The best time to spray

If your plan applies a product, Leaf checks the next five days of forecast hour by hour and picks the best 3 hours. One tap adds it to your calendar.

What it checks
  • Dry leaves, and at least 6 dry hours after (24 is best)
  • Wind under 10 mph, gusts under 15
  • 45 to 90 °F, or 85 °F with oil or sulfur, which burn leaves in heat
  • Early morning or evening only, if a product harms bees

Fixed rules in code, Open-Meteo's hourly forecast. The product label always wins.

Organic and pet-safe, enforced

Tick "organic only" or "pets or children" and any step that breaks it is removed, with the reason. The model is told, and code checks anyway.

What gets checked

Each product is matched against 33 active ingredients and labelled organic or not, its risk to pets and whether it harms bees. Leaf also warns when two steps clash (oil and sulfur burn leaves together) and says how long to keep pets off.

6 of 9 times Auto said "Confident", it was right.

These are hard photos: fruit, insects and odd problems from public-domain archives, not tidy leaf shots. Auto was right first time on 4 of 12 diseases, 3 of 6 pests and 1 of 6 other problems. It sent 10 of 24 to a lab, and in 9 of those the top answer was wrong. Quick said "Confident" on 7 and was right on only 3, which is why Auto is the default.

The caveats
  • Held out means nothing was tuned on these 24 photos and the run was done once. On the 52 photos Leaf was tuned on, Auto scored 58%, so the held-out number is the honest one.
  • 24 photos is small: one photo is about 4 points, and Quick and Auto tied on the first answer. Deep was not run on this split.
  • Answers are scored by name, so some near-misses count as wrong: "Iron deficiency" for iron chlorosis, "Septoria" for cercospora.
  • Late blight was missed again: the vision model sometimes reports a coating on it that isn't there.

Questions

Does it replace my extension office?

No. It tells you when you need one, and hands you a case summary to take along.

What happens to my photos and location?

Only small thumbnails are kept, for 30 days, under a private link. Your browser location is rounded before it is sent: to about 10 km for a diagnosis, and to about 1 km for a garden, which Leaf keeps to fetch your weather. You can delete a garden from its Today page, and gardens nobody opens for four months are deleted. A shared link never shows your notes, though the written summary of a case may repeat what they said.

Which plants does it know?

Common vegetables, fruit, roses and garden ornamentals. Weather-based disease rules cover tomato and potato. For anything unusual, expect "Likely" or a lab referral.

Where does it not work yet?

Radar covers US states only and often finds little, because extension sites mostly publish evergreen guides. Pest scouting by degree days is northern hemisphere only. Weather alerts are US only. Telegram needs a bot token that has not been set up yet, so it is hidden until it is.

Where do the numbers on this page come from?

From real runs, written down in the README and docs/LEAF_PLATFORM.md, with the date and what was tested. Estimates are labelled as estimates.

Is it free?

Yes. The project pays for the model and search calls, so the public demo has a daily limit. The code is MIT licensed.
